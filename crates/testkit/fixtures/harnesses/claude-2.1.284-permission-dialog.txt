
  Introduction

  A glacier is one of the slowest-moving forces on Earth, and it is also one of the most powerful. It forms where more snow falls in winter than melts in summer, year after year, until the accumulated weight transforms
  the lowest layers into ice that begins to flow under its own mass. Glaciers currently cover about ten percent of the planet's land surface. They hold roughly two-thirds of the world's fresh water. They have carved some
  of the most dramatic landscapes on Earth, including the fjords of Norway, the Great Lakes of North America, and the U-shaped valleys of the Alps. They also record the history of the atmosphere in their layers. Today
  they are retreating, and their retreat is one of the clearest physical signals of a warming climate. This essay looks at how glaciers form, how they move, how they shape land, what they reveal about the past, and what
  their decline means for the future.

  How Glaciers Form

  Every glacier begins as snow. When fresh snow falls, it is light and full of air, with a density of perhaps fifty to a hundred kilograms per cubic meter. In regions where summer melt does not remove the previous
  winter's snowfall, layers accumulate. The weight of the upper layers compresses the lower ones, and the delicate six-sided snowflakes break down into rounded grains. Snow that survives at least one summer is called
  firn, a granular material intermediate between snow and ice, with a density around four hundred to eight hundred kilograms per cubic meter.

  As burial continues, the firn is squeezed further. Air passages between the grains close off, trapping tiny bubbles of ancient atmosphere. Eventually the material reaches a density of about 830 kilograms per cubic
  meter and becomes glacial ice. The time this takes varies enormously. In a warm, wet maritime setting such as the coast of Alaska or New Zealand's Southern Alps, the transformation may take only a few years. In the
  cold, dry interior of Antarctica, where snowfall is scant, it can take several thousand years and require burial under a hundred meters or more of overlying snow.
