⏺ Fire Mountains: An Essay on Volcanoes

  Introduction

  Few natural features command as much awe, fear, and fascination as the volcano. A volcano is at once a mountain and a wound, a construction site and a demolition, a place where the interior of the planet reaches the
  surface and briefly reveals what lies beneath the ground we walk on. For most of human history, volcanoes were explained through myth. The Romans imagined the forge of Vulcan beneath Etna and the islands of the
  Mediterranean. Polynesian peoples told of Pele, the goddess who shaped and reshaped the Hawaiian Islands with lava. In Iceland, medieval writers described eruptions as the gateways to hell. Today we understand
  volcanoes through the language of plate tectonics, geochemistry, and physics, yet the sense of wonder has not diminished. If anything, deeper knowledge has made volcanoes more remarkable, because it shows how
  intimately they are tied to the workings of the entire Earth: its internal heat, the composition of its atmosphere, the history of its climate, and the fortunes of the life that lives on it.

  This essay explores volcanoes from several angles: what they are and how they form, the different styles of eruption, the landforms they build, the hazards they pose, the ways scientists study and forecast them, their
  influence on climate and life, their role in human history and culture, and the volcanic worlds beyond Earth. Together these themes show that volcanoes are not merely dramatic accidents of geology but central actors in
  the story of our planet.
