⏺ The Life of Rivers: Arteries of the Earth

  Introduction

  A river is one of the simplest ideas humans have ever had, and one of the hardest to pin down. We point at a line of moving water and say "the Nile" or "the Mississippi," as if the name referred to a single, fixed
  thing. It doesn't. The water in a river is never the same from one hour to the next. The channel shifts across decades. Its banks erode and rebuild. Its floods and droughts follow rhythms older than any human record. A
  river is less an object than a process: a continuous conversation between gravity, water, rock, and life.

  Rivers have shaped the planet's surface more than almost any other force. They have carved canyons, built deltas, and moved mountains, one grain of sediment at a time, to the sea. They have also shaped human history.
  Cities, empires, religions, and languages have grown along their banks. This essay looks at rivers from several angles: how they form and work physically, how they support ecosystems, how civilizations have depended on
  them, how we have altered them, and what their future may hold.
